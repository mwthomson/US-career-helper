## The Emotional Reality

Being told you are too old — whether explicitly or through the coded language of rejection — is one of the most personally devastating experiences in a job search. It strikes at something you cannot change.

Unlike a skills gap you can address or a presentation style you can improve, your age is immutable. When rejection is linked to age, it feels like a verdict on who you are, not what you did.

**For people made redundant after 20-30 years at one company, this is compounded:**
- You gave your career to an organization. That loyalty feels unrewarded.
- Your professional identity was built around a role and a company. Both are gone.
- Re-entering a job market that has changed beyond recognition is disorienting.
- Interview panels may be significantly younger than you. The power dynamic feels inverted.
- Your experience — the thing you have most of — is somehow being held against you.

**This is not weakness. This is a normal human response to a genuinely difficult situation.**

The guidance in this reference is designed to help you understand your rights, take practical action where possible, and — most importantly — not let the prejudice of some employers define your worth.

## US Age Discrimination Law: The ADEA

### The Core Protection

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. §§ 621-634, makes it unlawful for an employer to discriminate against a person who is 40 or older because of their age, in hiring, firing, pay, promotion, layoff, training, benefits, or any other term or condition of employment. It applies to private employers with 20 or more employees, unions, employment agencies, and most government employers.

**A narrow exception exists** where age is a "bona fide occupational qualification" (BFOQ) reasonably necessary to the job — this is rare, and courts read it narrowly (it has come up for a small number of safety-sensitive roles).

For the current text and EEOC guidance, see eeoc.gov/age-discrimination rather than relying on a summary here — specific thresholds and interpretations can shift with new case law and regulations.

### The Practical Reality of Proving Age Discrimination at Interview

The ADEA sets a **higher bar** for plaintiffs than most other US discrimination laws: since *Gross v. FBL Financial Services, Inc.*, 557 U.S. 167 (2009), a plaintiff must show age was the "but-for" cause of the employer's decision, not merely "a motivating factor." This makes ADEA claims genuinely harder to win than, for example, a race or sex discrimination claim under Title VII.

Two other well-established points from US case law are worth knowing:
- You do not need to show you were replaced by someone under 40 — only that you were replaced by someone **significantly younger** (*O'Connor v. Consolidated Coin Caterers Corp.*, 517 U.S. 308 (1996)).
- A decision based on a factor that merely correlates with age (such as years of service or pension status) is not automatically age discrimination — there has to be an actual link to age itself (*Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604 (1993)).

**Why it's hard in practice, regardless of the law:**

| Challenge | Why It Matters |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Interviews are private** | No independent witnesses. It becomes your word against theirs |
| **Decisions are inherently subjective** | Employers can almost always point to a non-discriminatory reason ("better cultural fit," "stronger presentation") |
| **Comparator evidence is limited** | You typically do not know who else applied, their age, or how they were scored |
| **"Something more" is generally required** | Simply being older and not getting the job is rarely enough on its own; you need additional evidence suggesting age was a factor |
| **Cost and emotional toll** | Filing an EEOC charge is free, but pursuing a case beyond that is a real time and emotional commitment, and most claimants represent themselves at the charge stage |

### What Evidence Would Strengthen a Claim

If you believe you have experienced age discrimination in recruitment, the following evidence is valuable:

**Document immediately after the interview:**
- Exact words used by interviewers (especially age-related comments)
- Questions asked that relate to age, retirement, or longevity
- The time, date, location, and names of those present
- Your qualifications versus what was required
- Any written communications (emails, rejection letters, feedback)

**Gather where possible:**
- The job advert (especially age-coded language)
- The job description and required qualifications
- Evidence of who was hired and their approximate age
- Evidence of the employer's hiring patterns (do they consistently hire younger?)
- The employer's equal opportunity policy (or lack thereof)
- Any admissions in correspondence

## Protections at the Interview and Recruitment Stage

**Protection begins before any employment relationship exists.** From the moment a job is advertised, the ADEA applies. A rejected job applicant can bring a claim without ever having been employed by that employer.

### What Is Covered

| Stage | What Is Protected |
|-------|------------------|
| **Job adverts** | Wording such as "young and dynamic," "recent graduate," "digital native," or "high energy" can indicate age preference |
| **Application forms** | Asking for date of birth is not automatically unlawful, but using it to filter candidates would be discriminatory |
| **Shortlisting** | Rejecting someone as "overqualified" where this is a proxy for age |
| **Interview conduct** | Inappropriate questions about age, retirement plans, or family circumstances |
| **Decision-making** | Basing hiring decisions on age-related assumptions about "cultural fit," "energy levels," or technological competence |

### Common Age-Coded Language in Job Adverts

| Phrase | Why It Is Problematic |
|--------|----------------------|
| "Young and dynamic team" | Implies older workers would not fit |
| "Recent graduate" | Directly excludes experienced candidates |
| "Digital native" | Implies a generational requirement |
| "2-5 years' experience" (with no upper limit justification) | Can exclude older workers, though may be justified for the role |
| "High energy environment" | Can be coded language for youth |
| "Fast-paced start-up culture" | May discourage older applicants |
| "Must be comfortable reporting to a younger manager" | Explicit age reference |

## If You Want to Pursue a Claim: Where to Start

This is deliberately a short pointer, not a step-by-step procedural guide — deadlines, forms, and agency practices change, and getting a specific one wrong is worse than not stating it. Start here:

1. **The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)** is the federal agency that enforces the ADEA. In almost all cases, you must file a "Charge of Discrimination" with the EEOC before you can sue. There are strict deadlines (180 days from the discriminatory act, sometimes extended to 300 days depending on your state) — confirm your exact deadline directly with the EEOC or an attorney rather than relying on a fixed number here.
   - eeoc.gov | 1-800-669-4000 | EEOC Public Portal for filing a charge online
2. **Your state's fair employment practices agency** (most states have one — often called a Civil Rights Commission or Human Rights Commission) can often take the same charge in parallel with the EEOC. Search "[your state] civil rights commission employment discrimination" to find yours.
3. **An employment attorney** — many offer a free initial consultation, and some take age discrimination cases on contingency. This is the right person to ask about your specific deadline, the strength of your evidence, and likely remedies (which under the ADEA can include back pay and, for willful violations, liquidated damages — but not the emotional-distress or punitive damages available under some other discrimination laws).

### A Framework for Deciding Whether to Pursue It

| Factor | Pursue a Claim If... | Focus Energy Elsewhere If... |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------------|
| **Evidence** | You have strong evidence (discriminatory comments, age-coded advert, written feedback mentioning age, pattern of young hires) | Evidence is limited to a feeling or suspicion |
| **Impact** | The discrimination was egregious or part of a clear pattern | It was a single rejection without strong indicators |
| **Emotional capacity** | You have the resilience and support to sustain a process that can take many months | The process would significantly harm your mental health or job search momentum |
| **Practical outcome** | You want to hold the employer accountable and/or seek back pay | You want to find a job as quickly as possible |
| **Systemic change** | You want to contribute to changing discriminatory practices | Your priority is your own career progress |

**Both choices are valid.** Choosing not to pursue a claim is not giving up. It is making a strategic decision about where your energy will have the most impact on your life.

## Building a Record Even If You Do Not Pursue a Claim

Even if you decide not to pursue individual rejections, maintaining a record has value:

- **Pattern recognition:** If you experience repeated indicators of age discrimination across multiple employers, the cumulative record may become relevant later
- **Personal clarity:** Documenting what happened separates facts from interpretations and protects your sense of reality
- **Future reference:** If circumstances change, having contemporaneous notes is far more valuable than relying on memory months later
- **Sharing patterns:** Organizations like AARP (aarp.org) track and campaign on age discrimination in the workplace and may be a useful place to share a pattern, alongside your state's civil rights agency

**What to record for each application:**
- Company name, role, date of application, date of rejection
- Stage reached (application, screen, interview, final)
- Any age-related indicators (advert language, interview questions, feedback)
- Your notes on what happened, written the same day
- Names and roles of people you interacted with
